# The six mistakes and the four slides

From Course 1, Critical Thinking at Work in the AI Era, Lesson 8.6: slides that trigger a decision. The same six mistakes, with the same names, are in the Deck Doctor game: https://santiagotacoronte.com/productivity-tips/bad-presentation-examples/

A usual deck goes background, context, methodology, analysis, conclusions. By the time the recommendation shows up, if it shows up, people are tired and the meeting is over time. The team built the deck to inform. The room needed a decision.

## The six mistakes

### 1. No decision asked
The deck ends on "Next steps", "Discussion", "Questions?" or "Thank you", and nowhere asks the room to approve, choose or sign something.
- Shows it: "Next steps: let's discuss."
- Gets it right: "Decision needed today: approve Teamplan, signed by Friday."
- Fix: end on the question: approve what, by when.

### 2. No recommendation
The conclusions sum up findings and never say what to do. The team knows the answer and keeps it to itself, so the room has to guess.
- Shows it: "Conclusions: both tools meet our core needs."
- Gets it right: "We recommend Teamplan, because it works offline at all three sites."
- Fix: say what you think they should do, and why.

### 3. No owner and no date
Nobody is named as owning the decision, and no date says when it's needed. Check the whole deck, including the title slide.
- Shows it: no name and no deadline anywhere.
- Gets it right: "Owner: the support lead. Decision by Friday."
- Fix: one owner, one date.

### 4. Key facts hidden
The facts that change the choice sit deep in the deck, after the background, or inside a slide about something else.
- Shows it: slide 8 of 11, in small print, "Two of three sites report unreliable internet. Rotaboard only works online."
- Gets it right: those facts have their own slide, right after the options.
- Fix: put the facts that inform the decision right after the options, where the room sees them.
- Not a mistake if the deck has no clear deciding fact yet: then ask the user (step 3), and note it.

### 5. Irrelevant information
Slides that don't help pick between the options: market overviews, methodology, long background, feature lists of options already ruled out.
- Shows it: "Market overview: twelve scheduling tools", for a choice between two.
- Test for each slide: if this slide vanished, would the choice change? If not, it goes to the appendix.
- Background slides on their own are a light case. Name the heaviest one (the one that takes up the room's time and helps the choice least).

### 6. Option missing
There is no "do nothing" or "keep what we have" option. Without it, nobody sees what each new option really adds, and sometimes doing nothing wins.
- Shows it: only Rotaboard and Teamplan, though slide 2 says shifts are planned today in a shared spreadsheet.
- Gets it right: "Option 3: keep the spreadsheet, €0."

## The four slides

1. **The decision.** One sentence, with the date it's needed. Write this sentence before any other slide. If it doesn't fit in one sentence, more slides won't help.
2. **The options.** Two or three, including doing nothing. Under each: the cost and the main risk. More than three makes people hesitate.
3. **The facts.** The facts that change the choice, from the user, with any deck numbers that inform it (for example the price gap).
4. **The recommendation.** What to do and why, the owner, the date. From the user.

Everything else goes to an appendix list. Nothing gets deleted, it just stops taking the room's time.

## Why the user brings the fact and the recommendation

An AI can list the options and spot buried facts, but it can't know which facts decide it for this team, or what this team should do. That judgment is the user's, and their name goes on the slide.
